Technology Strategy Optimization Director of Engineering, Yummy Corp

The platform worked, but cost per outlet wasn't falling as outlets were added. The unit economics didn't scale. Refactoring the entire technology platform was the only way to change that shape: the underlying issue was cost structure, and the platform was already technically sound.

Cost per outlet is the number that decides whether a ghost kitchen business works, and it's the one a technology organisation can move without anybody in the kitchens changing what they do. Each new outlet brings its own licence and its own integration, and it also adds another slice of operations headcount. The technology is a variable cost wearing a fixed cost's clothing, and growth makes the problem larger rather than cheaper, and every decision below was tested against whether it made the hundredth outlet cheaper than the tenth.

The platform itself is three products wearing one balance sheet. There's a SaaS layer for other ghost kitchen operators, centralised menu management across delivery platforms and order fulfilment tied to raw-materials consumption, with the ERP integration below sitting under both. There are also self-service apps for food court customers: ordering kiosks, online self-order and queue management. None of that shows up in the 80% figure at the end of this, since it's the surface area the refactor had to keep working while it changed underneath.

Migrating AWS to GCP and repurchasing cloud-native ERP. Repurchasing the existing ERP, instead of migrating it, was the harder call and the right one. Carrying a self-managed system into a new environment would have moved the licence and operational burden intact, still unresolved.

Developer experience came next, treated as a cost lever: GitHub Actions, Terraform and Go, leaning on managed GCP services wherever running something ourselves earned nothing. Release cycle time is the metric that compounds: everything downstream of it gets cheaper when it drops.

And the org chart, because the expense line is mostly people. Trainee and upskilling programmes to build capability in-house, with outsourcing reserved for genuinely specialised work, since buying permanent headcount for a temporary specialism only adds to the budget without adding to capability.

An 80% reduction isn't one saving, and the figure shouldn't be read as a single clever move. It is a licensing model replaced, a self-managed estate handed to managed services, an outsourcing line brought in-house, and a release process that stopped consuming people. None of those individually gets close. They compound because each one removes work the next one would otherwise have had to keep paying for. That's also why the order mattered, since doing the org chart first would have meant training a team to operate infrastructure I was about to delete.
