Most of what follows is the first problem being solved by fixing the second, because a programme that's late for structural reasons can't be rescued by pushing harder on the schedule.

Restaurant Technology Platform

Product Management Director of Engineering, Yummy Corp

Fast-tracked product–market fit for front and back of house technology in the restaurant industry. Three systems, built in the order that let each one pay for the next.

The sequence was the strategy. Rapid outlet growth doesn't leave room to build three systems in parallel and integrate them at the end, and it punishes any component that can't be rolled out to a live kitchen during service. So each system had to be independently useful on the day it shipped, and each one had to lower the cost of the next. Merely making it possible wasn't the bar. That ruled out the platform-first approach, where nothing is visible to an operator until the foundations are finished.

Front of house came first: point-of-sale, kitchen display system, loyalty programme and real-time analytics. That gave every later system trustworthy transaction data from day one, which mattered once reporting had to be built on top of it rather than bolted on after the fact.

The IoT layer was designed alongside Operations, because the waste it targeted showed up on the kitchen floor, not in a code review. An ESP32-based system handled temperature monitoring and energy metering, with EMQX as the broker, and it also ran AI-on-the-edge detection. Inference runs on the device, because a kitchen with intermittent connectivity still needs its cold storage watched, and streaming raw sensor data to the cloud just to answer a question the device can answer itself doesn't buy anything.

Edge inference isn't free, and the price is paid somewhere other than the cloud bill. Models and firmware have to reach devices sitting behind a restaurant's network, which means an update path that works unattended, and a device that decides for itself is a device you can't debug by reading a log in one place. I took that trade because the failure it protects against is the one that actually costs money: cold storage going out of range during an outage is spoilage. I didn't route that alert over the same link most likely to be down during the outage it's meant to catch, since that link carried only questions that could wait for an answer, and their data went to the cloud instead.

I also took ownership of product management directly, driving the roadmap for B2B value-added services and loyalty features. I owned that roadmap instead of receiving it, which made the sequencing above enforceable. The order systems get built in is a product decision first and an engineering one second. A CTO who only holds the engineering half of that decision can advise on the order without being the one accountable for it.

Ghost Kitchen Startup

Technology Strategy Optimization Director of Engineering, Yummy Corp

The platform worked, but cost per outlet wasn't falling as outlets were added. The unit economics didn't scale. Refactoring the entire technology platform was the only way to change that shape: the underlying issue was cost structure, and the platform was already technically sound.

Cost per outlet is the number that decides whether a ghost kitchen business works, and it's the one a technology organisation can move without anybody in the kitchens changing what they do. Each new outlet brings its own licence and its own integration, and it also adds another slice of operations headcount. The technology is a variable cost wearing a fixed cost's clothing, and growth makes the problem larger rather than cheaper, and every decision below was tested against whether it made the hundredth outlet cheaper than the tenth.

The platform itself is three products wearing one balance sheet. There's a SaaS layer for other ghost kitchen operators, centralised menu management across delivery platforms and order fulfilment tied to raw-materials consumption, with the ERP integration below sitting under both. There are also self-service apps for food court customers: ordering kiosks, online self-order and queue management. None of that shows up in the 80% figure at the end of this, since it's the surface area the refactor had to keep working while it changed underneath.

Migrating AWS to GCP and repurchasing cloud-native ERP. Repurchasing the existing ERP, instead of migrating it, was the harder call and the right one. Carrying a self-managed system into a new environment would have moved the licence and operational burden intact, still unresolved.

Developer experience came next, treated as a cost lever: GitHub Actions, Terraform and Go, leaning on managed GCP services wherever running something ourselves earned nothing. Release cycle time is the metric that compounds: everything downstream of it gets cheaper when it drops.

And the org chart, because the expense line is mostly people. Trainee and upskilling programmes to build capability in-house, with outsourcing reserved for genuinely specialised work, since buying permanent headcount for a temporary specialism only adds to the budget without adding to capability.

An 80% reduction isn't one saving, and the figure shouldn't be read as a single clever move. It is a licensing model replaced, a self-managed estate handed to managed services, an outsourcing line brought in-house, and a release process that stopped consuming people. None of those individually gets close. They compound because each one removes work the next one would otherwise have had to keep paying for. That's also why the order mattered, since doing the org chart first would have meant training a team to operate infrastructure I was about to delete.

  • 80% reduction in ICT-related expenses
  • 50% faster release cycle

Multinational Retail Company

Digital Transformation Chief Technology Officer, Sophie Paris Indonesia

A B2B2C e-commerce transformation three years behind schedule, and a USD 3 million ERP and WMS migration that wasn't going to land, so the first job was triage.

The rescue turned on scope. The programme was replacing ERP and WMS together, an ambitious combination for a project already three years late. I implemented the new WMS and kept the legacy ERP for financials and manufacturing. The warehouse was where the pain and the return actually were. The financial modules already worked. That split is what realised the ROI on a project already written off, and it's the decision I would point to first — finishing meant stopping part of the work.

The hard part of that decision had nothing to do with the technology. A programme three years in has a sunk cost that everyone can name and a set of commitments people have already defended in front of a board. Proposing to deliver less than was promised reads as conceding failure at exactly the moment nobody wants to. I made it possible to carry by separating the two systems on evidence: the warehouse was where the operational pain and the measurable return both sat, and the financial modules weren't the thing anyone was complaining about. Pulling back on the part that was already working was the easier case to make, so that's the direction I argued.

Re-engineering the IT function was the other half. A programme that's missed for three years is usually organised to produce status reports, regardless of who's staffed on it. Changing who decided what, and shortening the distance between a problem being seen and being fixed, is what gave a nine-month plan its credibility. The same team largely delivered it.

The transformation then completed in nine months after three years of not completing, on an IT function re-engineered to deliver.

From there the work compounded on what was already fixed. The distributor point-of-sale system needed refactoring, and the e-commerce, point-of-sale and loyalty channels needed one unified omnichannel UX, which is what moved customer lifetime value. Giving a distributor a 360 view took consolidating disparate sales, stock and assortment data into PowerBI dashboards. That's the reporting nobody could assemble while the data sat in separate systems.

Finally, harmonising IT systems across affiliates in Vietnam, Malaysia and the Philippines with a global cross-functional team, so regional expansion had something to expand onto. Social commerce across Indonesia, Vietnam and the Philippines scaled to 1.5 million monthly active users.

Browser-Local Semantic Search

Applied AI This site

The search on the front page of this site answers questions about these projects by running a sentence embedding model inside the visitor's own browser. There is no inference server and no API key — the question itself never leaves the tab. It's deliberately small, because the point was building the thing end to end. Describing having once specified it wouldn't have done that.

all-MiniLM-L6-v2, quantised to 8-bit, executed by ONNX Runtime on WebAssembly through Transformers.js. Quantisation is what makes the model shippable at all — full precision is four times the weight for a retrieval quality difference this corpus can't resolve. The runtime runs single-threaded because GitHub Pages can't send the COOP and COEP headers that SharedArrayBuffer requires, so the threaded build would silently fall back anyway. I measured crossOriginIsolated in the deployed page. Assuming it held wasn't good enough.

The model and the runtime are served from this origin, and so are the weights, because vendoring only works if nothing quietly reaches outside the page. The obvious build fetches them from a model hub on first use. That would mean pinning a CDN script by SRI digest in one breath and then pulling tens of megabytes of unpinned executable WebAssembly from a third party in the next — the same integrity problem an order of magnitude larger. Vendoring costs repository size and buys a page with no third-party requests at all. I confirmed that by watching the network panel, not by reading the source.

Retrieval is an exhaustive cosine scan. A few dozen passages at 384 dimensions is under ten thousand multiply-accumulates — microseconds, and four orders of magnitude below the cost of embedding the question that precedes it. An HNSW graph would add a dependency and a build artefact just to optimise the part that's already free. It's an elevator installed for one flight of stairs. It would also cost recall, since an exhaustive scan has none of that tradeoff to begin with. Knowing where that crossover sits is the engineering. Reaching for the vector database before that crossover arrives adds complexity for no benefit.

The index is generated by a page. Embeddings are precomputed and committed, because asking every visitor's device to embed the whole corpus before answering the first question is a cost paid thousands of times to save paying it once. That risks letting the index drift from the prose it describes. So the generator runs in a browser, against the same vendored runtime and weights the site ships, which makes the vectors the ones the live code would have produced by construction. A build on a server couldn't promise that: outside a browser the runtime's backend registry is empty, and a vector computed by a different stack doesn't raise an error, it just retrieves worse. A check in CI then fails the build if any committed passage stops appearing on this page.

This approach stops working somewhere, and that's the part worth knowing. The search itself is nearly free. The download is what bounds it. Twenty megabytes is a defensible one-time cost on a page someone chose to interrogate, and it would be indefensible on a page that had to answer before the visitor asked for anything. The linear scan holds to a few thousand passages on this hardware. Past that, the crossover with an approximate index finally arrives, and the honest move is to take the dependency I argued against above. And a 384-dimension general-purpose embedding retrieves topics well and proper nouns poorly, so a question naming one specific system is better served by find-in-page on the portfolio. Knowing which of those limits applies first is what makes this design workable here without making it a general-purpose solution.

The accessibility constraints shaped this code the most. The load button can't use the disabled property, because disabling the control somebody just pressed moves keyboard focus to the document body, and here it would strand them there for the length of a multi-second download. It sets an ARIA state and guards re-entry with a flag instead. Results are announced through a live region, because focus deliberately stays where the visitor put it. These are the decisions a demo never shows, the ones that determine whether anyone can actually use the feature.

  • No inference cost and no data egress — the question never leaves the tab
  • ~25ms per query after a one-time model load
  • Zero third-party requests at runtime

Leading the team

Engineering Leadership Teams of up to fifty, across four markets

A global technology team of up to fifty, distributed across Indonesia, Vietnam, Malaysia and the Philippines. Distributed across markets is a different problem from distributed across cities. The teams were attached to affiliates with their own systems and their own regulators, each with commercial priorities of its own, so the work meant building software together, and it meant agreeing what was genuinely shared and what only looked shared. I spent most of that time negotiating with those affiliates, not harmonising systems.

Build capability in-house, with a stated exception. Trainee and upskilling programmes were how the ghost kitchen cost structure actually changed. They are the part of that story I would defend hardest, because they are the part that kept paying after I left. Outsourcing was reserved for genuinely specialised work. A specialism you need once doesn't justify permanent headcount, and hiring for it anyway is how a technology budget ratchets upward in a way nobody can later point at. I've built offshore IT services businesses earlier in my career, so I've sat on the vendor side of that conversation too. A vendor has a harder time selling me on headcount I don't need.

Measure the delivery system: DORA metrics, read as properties of the pipeline. Release cycle time is the one I watch first because it compounds: everything downstream of a shorter cycle gets cheaper, and a team that can ship on a Tuesday afternoon makes decisions a team batching for a monthly window simply doesn't get to make. When those numbers get used to rank individuals they stop measuring anything, so I hold that line explicitly when reviewing them.

I read the diffs, and I'm careful about what that's for. Staying close to the code is how I keep an accurate model of what is expensive, which is what makes an estimate arguable on its merits. It isn't a licence to review my way into other people's work. Reviewing everything to stay in control slows the team down, and that's the failure mode I watch for in myself.

How I decide

Operating Principles Drawn from the projects above

None of this was written down as a philosophy in advance. It's what the four projects above have in common, noticed only afterwards, not a plan followed from the start.

Find the cost structure before writing the plan. The ghost kitchen looked like a platform refactor, but the real problem was unit economics. The retail programme looked like a delivery problem, but the real problem was scope. Same diagnosis, different symptom. Get the diagnosis wrong and no amount of good execution fixes the plan that follows from it.

The fastest way to finish a programme that's years late is usually to stop doing part of it. That's descoping. It's a delivery technique, not a retreat, though it looks like one, which is why it's unpopular and why it has to be argued on evidence about where the return actually sits, argued early enough that the saving is real.

Prefer the boring component and justify the interesting one. This shows up as managed services over running it ourselves, and as retrieval over fine-tuning, for the same reason each time: the boring option had already beaten the alternative on a number I could name before deciding. The exhaustive scan behind the search on this site is the same call, made once more, against a vector database that would only have paid for itself later. A sophisticated tool only wins the argument when it beats the simple one on a number I can name in advance, and without that number it doesn't get adopted here.

I checked whether cross-origin isolation was available in the deployed page before concluding threads were unavailable, and I read the transfer sizes straight off the network panel, live, not off the files sitting on disk. That's the whole discipline: measure the thing rather than assume it. Most architecture arguments are settled by one measurement that nobody bothered to take, and it's usually cheaper to take that measurement than to keep arguing without one.

Leave it maintainable by whoever is next. A system that only works while its author is present hasn't been finished. This site is the small version of the same instinct: no build step and no package manager, so it's still editable years from now without reviving a toolchain. A check enforces every invariant that would otherwise be easy to break by accident.
